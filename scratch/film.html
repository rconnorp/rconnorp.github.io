Section 01

Cinematography — The Language of Light

Before I started shooting video at the Sorensen Center, I thought of cinematography as aesthetics — beautiful frames, interesting angles, the visual surface of a film. The more I actually work behind a camera, the more I understand it as something closer to architecture. Light shapes space. Space shapes behavior. The way a room is lit tells an audience what to feel before anyone speaks a word.

Roger Deakins, widely considered one of the greatest living cinematographers, has spent decades articulating this with unusual precision. His insight — that cinematography is often more about the absence of light than its presence — reframes the whole discipline. You're not adding light to a scene; you're carving it, removing what's unnecessary, letting shadow do the emotional work.

The physicality of film — a medium that demands intentionality by design.

What Deakins teaches is patience: the willingness to wait for the right quality of light, to build a setup slowly, to trust that a simple motivated source will always outperform an overcrowded rig. That discipline translates directly into how I approach production work. Every video I make at the Sorensen Center starts with the question: what does this room already want to do with light?

Section 02

Editing — The Invisible Art

There's a popular line attributed to various editors that a film is written three times: once in the script, once on set, and once in the edit. The third draft is the one audiences see. It's also the most ruthless — because the edit has no attachment to what anything cost to produce. If a scene doesn't serve the whole, it goes.

Walter Murch, who cut Apocalypse Now and The English Patient, argued that the best cut is the one the audience doesn't notice — a transition so well-timed that it feels like a thought the viewer was already having. That's the standard I hold my own editing to, even on a two-minute institutional video: no cut that draws attention to itself.

The theater — where editing either holds or loses its audience.

Foundations of Visual Storytelling

  1. Composition — Frame as Argument
    • Rule of thirds as a starting point, not a rule
    • Negative space as emotional weight
    • Symmetry as control; asymmetry as unease
    • Leading lines and where the eye naturally travels
  2. Editing — Time as Material
    • Continuity cuts vs. montage logic
    • Pacing: the relationship between cut length and emotional tempo
    • The L-cut and J-cut — letting sound precede or trail the image
    • Murch's rule of six: emotion, story, rhythm, eye trace, 2D plane, 3D space
  3. Sound — The Other Half
    • Diegetic vs. non-diegetic audio
    • Room tone: the silence that isn't silent
    • Score as subtext — what the music tells you the image won't
Section 03

Storytelling — What Survives the Cut

Finance and filmmaking share an uncomfortable truth: most of the work is invisible in the final product. A well-structured financing package is only visible in the stability it enables; a well-structured scene is only visible in the emotion it produces. The craft is in the architecture, not the ornament.

What draws me to entertainment media as a vertical — specifically the economics of theatrical exhibition, streaming ROI, and IP valuation — is precisely this: the business layer and the creative layer are inseparable. A studio's slate decisions are also creative decisions. A distribution deal shapes which stories get told. Understanding both sides of that equation is the whole point.

"The enemy of art is the absence of limitations."
— Orson Welles

That quote applies as much to a financing structure as it does to a screenplay. Constraints force clarity. The best financial models and the best films both know exactly what they're trying to do — and ruthlessly cut everything that doesn't serve that.
